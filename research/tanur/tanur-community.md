## 1. Demographic Profile (Census of India 2011)

### 1.1 Key Statistical Metrics
- **Administrative Jurisdiction:** Tanur Municipality (upgraded Nov 1, 2015 from erstwhile Tanur Grama Panchayat).
- **Total Local Body Population (2011):** **69,534**
- **Census Town (CT) Statistical Unit Population:** **44,973** (represents the central urbanized core of the erstwhile panchayat at the time of the 2011 enumeration).
- **Gender Breakdown (Census Town Data):**
  - Male Population: **21,452** (47.7%)
  - Female Population: **23,521** (52.3%)
- **Sex Ratio:** **1,096 females per 1,000 males** (higher than the national average and surpassing the Kerala state average of 1,084).
- **Child Population (0–6 years):** 6,750 (approx. 15% of total CT population).
- **Child Sex Ratio (0–6 years):** 953 girls per 1,000 boys.
- **Literacy Rate:** **91.18%**
  - Male Literacy: **94.15%**
  - Female Literacy: **88.55%**
- **Households:** Approximately 11,600 occupied residential houses across the municipality.

### 1.2 Administrative Delimitation Clarity
- **Tanur Municipality:** Governs **19.49 sq. km** of contiguous urbanized coastal territory divided into **45 electoral wards**.
- **Important Distinction:** Tanur Municipality must NOT be confused with the **Tanur Block Panchayat**, which is a separate intermediate tier of rural local self-governance headquartered nearby that administers five adjacent Grama Panchayats:
  1. *Thanalur* (താനാളൂർ)
  2. *Ozhur* (ഒഴൂർ)
  3. *Niramaruthur* (നിറമരുതൂർ)
  4. *Ponmundam* (പൊൻമുണ്ടം)
  5. *Cheriyamundam* (ചെറിയമുണ്ടം)

## 2. Social Fabric & Inter-Faith Harmony

### 2.1 Communal Harmony & Co-Existence
- Tanur exhibits a demographic mosaic comprising a Muslim majority alongside substantial Hindu communities (including Thiyya, Nair, Ezhava, Dheevara, Mukkuva, and Brahmin families), with historical Christian traces dating back to Portuguese times.
- The town has maintained a long tradition of mutual inter-communal respect. For example, during the Shobhaparambu Kalankari festival and local mosque Uroos events, community members from diverse religious backgrounds actively participate in civic arrangements, cultural processions, and commercial trade stalls.

### 2.2 The Fisherfolk Community
- The coastal fringe of Tanur (from the Harbour through Tanur Beach to Ottumpuram) is inhabited by traditional marine fishing families.
- **Occupational Hierarchy & Traditions:**
  - *Srank (Skipper/Captain):* Senior navigator possessing generational knowledge of ocean currents, celestial markers, sandbanks, and fish schooling patterns.
  - *Crew (Khalasis / Thozhilalikal):* Skilled seafaring deckhands handling nets, lines, and outboard motors.
  - *Auctioneers & Middlemen (Tharakans):* Licensed middlemen who facilitate the dawn auctions at the harbour landing wharves.
- **Off-Shore Cycles:** Traditional fishermen launch before dawn (3:00 AM – 4:30 AM) or operate overnight shifts depending on target species (mackerel, sardines, kingfish, prawns, squid).
- **Monsoon Trawl Ban Period:** During the annual 52-day monsoon marine fishing ban (June–July) enforced by the Department of Fisheries, coastal communities pivot to net mending, boat maintenance, near-shore non-mechanized craft, and community cooperative assistance programs.

### 2.3 Kudumbashree & Women’s Micro-Enterprises
- The **Kudumbashree Community Development Society (CDS)** of Tanur Municipality operates dozens of neighborhood groups (NHGs / Ayalkoottams).
- Prominent female-led collective enterprises include:
  1. Hygienic solar dry-fish processing and modern packaging.
  2. Marine fish retail outlets managed by women’s cooperatives.
  3. Organic vegetable cultivation on fallow coastal land.
  4. Garment making, tailoring, and traditional Malabar catering units.

## 3. The Gulf Diaspora (Pravasi Community)

### 3.1 Economic and Social Impact
- Similar to much of coastal Malappuram, almost every extended household in Tanur has one or more members employed across the GCC countries (UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Oman, Kuwait, Bahrain).
- **Remittance Inflows:** Foreign inward remittances have played a transformative role in Tanur's modern economy over the past four decades, driving:
  - The transition from thatched cottages to durable, modern multi-storey houses.
  - Substantial investments in children's higher and professional education.
  - Growth in retail commercial complexes, private clinics, supermarkets, and vehicle ownership.
  - Philanthropic funding for educational institutions (such as colleges and orphanages), free dialysis centers, and palliative care initiatives.
